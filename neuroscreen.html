Owlet Mobile Neurological Screening Platform

Problem · Why this matters

  • Early identification improves developmental outcomes, yet current screening methods are often slow, subjective, and difficult to access.
  • Research shows that eye-tracking and AI can detect early neurological signals with high accuracy, but these tools remain limited to clinical settings.
  • Owlet brings these methods into an accessible mobile experience, enabling earlier and more objective screening for families.

My Role & Contributions

I own the product and UX framing for Owlet—from problem definition through flows for users.

  • Designed end-to-end user flows for caregivers (setup, screening, reassurance)
  • Led data-collection UX: prompts, timing, error states, and recovery when sensors or camera conditions fail—critical for trustworthy signal.
  • Defined how results are presented so outputs feel credible and legible—balancing honesty about limits with actionable clarity for non-experts.

Research insights

  • Early detection improves outcomes. Children benefit significantly from earlier diagnosis and intervention.
  • Current screening is unreliable and inaccessible. Existing tools are subjective, inconsistent, and often unavailable to families without specialist access.
  • Technology can do better—but isn’t widely available. Advances in eye-tracking and AI enable accurate early detection, but are still limited to clinical environments.

Design Challenges

Depth of thinking matters more than a roadmap checklist—these tensions shaped the direction of the product.

  • Designing for infants—the indirect user. Every screen must work for caregivers while encoding behaviors the system needs from a child who cannot follow instructions.
  • Caregiver usability vs. clinical rigor. The experience must feel achievable at home without diluting protocols to the point of useless data.
  • Sensitive health data by default. Minimizing collection, surfacing consent and control, and avoiding fear-based language when families are already stressed.

Key Decisions & Tradeoffs

Explicit tradeoffs recruiters and hiring managers look for—documented here the same way as on my shipped work.

Simplicity vs. data accuracy Fewer steps reduce drop-off but can skip calibration that improves signal. The product bias is toward fewer, higher-quality captures over rushed completions.
Real-time feedback vs. processing reliability Live cues can reassure caregivers; heavy real-time computation risks instability on consumer hardware. Owlet favors dependable capture first, then progressive disclosure of status.
Clinical detail vs. caregiver readability Clinicians need parameters and context; families need empathy and clarity. The UI separates expert layers while keeping a single coherent story.
Speed to insight vs. validation burden Early development prioritizes traceability and ethical guardrails over promising speed we cannot yet defend empirically.
